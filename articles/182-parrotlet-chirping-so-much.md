# Why Is My Parrotlet Chirping So Much? Contact Calls, Boredom and Screaming

For a bird the size of a plum, a parrotlet can make a remarkable amount of noise. Most of it is one of three things: **contact calling, excitement, or "pay attention to me"**.

## 1. Contact calling (the most common)

In the wild, flock members call back and forth constantly to confirm where everyone is. In a flat, you are the flock.

Signs it's contact calling:
- Starts the moment you **leave the room**
- Stops when you **come back or answer**
- Gets louder the further away you go
- Happens most at **dawn and dusk** — flock-call times

**What to do:** answer it. A short whistle or a spoken "I'm here" from the other room usually settles the bird faster than silence, because the call has done its job. What makes it worse is running back into the room every time — that teaches the bird that noise summons you.

## 2. Excitement and fascination

Parrotlets latch onto specific sounds and objects: a crunching bag, running water, a particular ringtone, a toy bell. The chirping that comes with it is fast, bright and comes with bobbing, flitting and fluffed cheeks. Nothing to fix.

## 3. Demand and boredom

This is the one worth changing. A bird with a bare cage, no foraging and a short, predictable day fills the gap with sound. Signs:

- Repetitive, **monotonous** calling with no trigger
- Worse when the cage is in a quiet corner, or facing a wall
- Rising sharply if you react to it every time

**What helps:**
- **Foraging:** food wrapped in paper, hidden in cardboard, shredded toys, chew material replaced often
- **Routine out-of-cage time** at the same times each day, so the bird isn't calling to negotiate
- **Rotating toys** weekly rather than leaving the same four
- **Cage placed** where household life happens, but not under constant bright light
- **12 hours of dark, quiet sleep.** Short-slept parrotlets are noticeably louder and nippier.

## Chirping that signals a problem

- **Sudden, sustained alarm calling**: look for a cat, a bird of prey outside the window, a new object
- **Screaming with cage-bar biting or feather picking**: stress or frustration; review space, sleep and stimulation
- **Chirping plus tail bobbing, clicking breathing, fluffed daytime sleeping**: possible illness — avian vet
- **Hormonal calling** in spring, often with a nest-site fixation: shorten the day, remove nest-like spaces, cut rich food

## What not to do

- **Don't shout back.** Parrotlets escalate to match volume; it becomes a game.
- **Don't cover the cage as punishment.**
- **Don't reward the loudest call** with the thing the bird wants. Wait for a quiet moment, then come in.

## Is the noise level normal?

Parrotlets are not quiet birds, but they are high-pitched rather than loud like a conure or cockatoo. A single bird typically chirps in bursts through the day, with big peaks early and late. If it's constant from dawn to dusk with no gaps, treat it as boredom or stress rather than a personality trait.

## FAQ

**Do parrotlets get quieter with age?**
A settled adult with a reliable routine is usually calmer than a young bird, but they stay vocal.

**Should I get a second parrotlet to keep the first company?**
It can reduce contact calling, but parrotlets are territorial and pairs often bond to each other instead of you. Housing needs careful introduction and more space.

**Why does my parrotlet chirp at night?**
Something is disturbing the sleep: light, noise, or a shadow. Parrotlets need a dark, quiet 12-hour night.

**Is constant chirping a sign of a happy bird?**
Bright, varied chatter usually is. Monotonous, repetitive calling is more often boredom or frustration.
