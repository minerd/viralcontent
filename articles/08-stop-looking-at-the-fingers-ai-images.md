# Stop Counting Fingers: How to Actually Tell If an Image Is AI in 2026

A couple of years ago, spotting an AI image was almost a party trick. Count the fingers. Look for melted text in the background. Check if the earrings match.

That advice is mostly dead. Current image models get hands right most of the time, write readable signs, and handle reflections well enough to fool a quick glance. If you're still looking for six fingers, you're going to miss most fakes.

The good news: you don't need to be a pixel detective. The better method is to stop staring at the image and start checking where it came from.

## The 60-second check

When an image makes you feel something strong (shock, anger, "wow, no way"), run these four steps before you share it.

### 1. Who posted it first? (15 seconds)

Not who you saw it from. Who posted it *first*.

A real photo of a real event almost always has a traceable source: a news outlet, a photographer, a named person who was there. AI fakes tend to appear out of nowhere, usually on accounts that post a lot of viral content and very little else.

If you can't find anyone who claims to have taken the photo, that's a red flag on its own.

### 2. Reverse image search (20 seconds)

Use Google Lens, Bing Visual Search, or TinEye. Upload the image or paste its link.

You're looking for:
- **Older copies.** If an "image from yesterday's storm" shows up in results from 2023, it's either old or recycled.
- **Fact-checks.** Often someone has already debunked it.
- **The original.** Sometimes the real photo exists and the viral one is an edited version.

### 3. Check for Content Credentials (10 seconds)

More cameras, phones, and AI tools now attach **Content Credentials** (based on a standard called C2PA) that record how an image was made or edited. Some platforms show a small "CR" label or an "AI info" tag.

You can also upload an image to the Content Credentials verify site (contentcredentials.org/verify) to see if it carries this information.

Honest caveat: the absence of credentials doesn't prove an image is fake. Screenshots and re-uploads strip them all the time. But when they *are* present, they're one of the strongest signals you have.

### 4. Does the story make sense? (15 seconds)

Ask:
- Would there be other photos of this if it were real? A celebrity at a public event, a disaster, a protest: there'd be dozens of angles. One lone perfect image is suspicious.
- Is the image *too* perfect? Perfect lighting, perfect composition, perfect emotional moment, in a chaotic situation.
- Is it confirming exactly what a certain group of people wants to believe? Fakes are built to be shared, and outrage is shared most.

## The visual clues that still (sometimes) work

Pixel-level clues aren't reliable anymore, but a few still help as a *second* check:

- **Background people and objects.** Models focus on the main subject. Faces in the crowd, text on distant signs, and fence railings still go strange more often than the foreground does.
- **Physics.** Shadows pointing different ways, reflections that don't match, water that behaves oddly.
- **That "smooth" look.** A lot of AI images have a slightly waxy, over-polished finish, especially on skin. It's a vibe, not proof.

Treat these as reasons to investigate, not verdicts.

## What about AI detector tools?

There are plenty of websites that claim to tell you whether an image is AI-generated. Some are decent. None are perfect, and they can be wrong in both directions: flagging real photos as fake and missing obvious fakes.

Use them as one opinion, never the final answer. The source and context checks above are more reliable than any detector score.

## The simplest rule

If you can't figure out where an image came from, don't share it as real. You can still share it with "not sure if this is real," which is honestly one of the most useful things you can add to the internet right now.

## FAQ

**Can you always tell if an image is AI-generated?**
No. Some AI images are now impossible to identify by looking alone. That's why checking the source matters more than inspecting the pixels.

**Does Google label AI images?**
Google and several other platforms show "AI info" or similar labels when an image carries the right metadata. Many AI images don't carry it, or lose it when they're re-uploaded.

**Are AI hands still wrong?**
Sometimes, but much less often than in earlier models. Don't rely on hands.

**Is it illegal to post AI images?**
Generally no, but using AI images to impersonate someone, commit fraud, or create non-consensual intimate content is illegal in many places, and laws are expanding.
