# Why Does My Cockatiel Sing at Night? (And How to Help Them Sleep)

Cockatiels are daytime birds. They wake with the sun, chatter all day, and are supposed to settle down when it gets dark. So when yours starts whistling their favorite tune at 11 p.m., something's usually keeping them up.

The good news: night singing is mostly harmless and usually fixable by adjusting their environment.

## Why cockatiels sing at night

**1. Light in the room**
The most common cause. TV screens, lamps, streetlights through the window, even a phone screen can make a cockatiel think it's still daytime. Cockatiels take their cues from light.

**2. You're still up**
Cockatiels are flock birds, and you're their flock. If they can hear you talking, watching TV or moving around, they may want to join in.

**3. A changed routine**
New house, new room, daylight saving changes, a different bedtime. Changes can throw off their internal clock for a while.

**4. Hormones**
Male cockatiels are the main singers, and singing is part of courtship. When hormones are high, some males sing more, at all hours. Long days (lots of light) can encourage hormonal behavior.

**5. Contentment**
Some cockatiels just softly chatter or sing a little as they settle down, similar to a sleepy mumble. Short, quiet singing that fades out is usually nothing to worry about.

## Singing vs night frights: know the difference

Night frights are something else entirely, and they're important to recognize.

**Night fright:** a sudden panic in the dark. The bird thrashes, flaps wildly, and may crash into the cage bars. Cockatiels are especially prone to them. It can cause injuries like broken blood feathers.

If you hear **flapping, crashing or panicked noises** rather than singing:
- Turn on a soft light
- Speak calmly until they settle
- Check for bleeding or injured feathers
- Consider a **dim night light** near the cage long-term

## How to help your cockatiel sleep

Cockatiels are commonly recommended to get **around 10–12 hours of darkness and quiet** each night.

- **Move the cage** to a quieter room away from the TV and household activity, if possible.
- **Block outside light** with curtains, or use a breathable cage cover (make sure there's airflow).
- **Keep a consistent bedtime.** Same time every night helps them settle.
- **Use a very dim night light** if your bird has had night frights. Many cockatiels do better with a little light than total darkness.
- **Reduce long days** if hormonal behavior seems high. Shorter light periods can help calm hormones.

## When to talk to a vet

Night singing on its own isn't a health issue. But see an avian vet if you notice:
- Changes in droppings, appetite or weight
- Fluffed-up posture during the day
- Breathing noises, tail bobbing
- Excessive hormonal behaviors in females, like repeated egg laying

## FAQ

**Is it normal for cockatiels to sing at night?**
Occasional soft singing as they settle is normal. Regular night singing usually means light, noise, routine changes or hormones are keeping them awake.

**How many hours of sleep does a cockatiel need?**
Many avian care guides recommend about 10–12 hours of dark, quiet time.

**Should I cover my cockatiel's cage at night?**
Many owners do, as long as there's airflow. Some cockatiels prone to night frights do better with a partial cover and a dim night light.

**What is a night fright?**
A sudden panic in the dark where a bird thrashes in its cage. Cockatiels are especially prone to them.
