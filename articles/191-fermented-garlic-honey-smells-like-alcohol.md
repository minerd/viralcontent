# Fermented Garlic Honey Smells Like Alcohol? Why It Happens and Is It Safe

You open the jar after a couple of weeks and it smells less like garlic and honey and more like **cider, wine or beer**. Nearly every page on fermented garlic honey is a recipe; almost none say what the boozy smell means.

**It means yeast is fermenting the sugar into alcohol.** That happens when the mixture gets too watery — and it's usually fixable.

## Why alcohol appears

Honey on its own won't ferment: at roughly **17% water** it's too dry for microbes to work. Garlic cloves are mostly water, and honey pulls that water out by osmosis. Once the mixture passes about **19–20% water**, wild yeasts wake up and do what yeast does — convert sugar to **alcohol and CO₂**.

So the smell is a sign of one of these:

- **Too much garlic for the amount of honey** (lots of cloves, thin layer of honey)
- **Water added**, or honey that was already thin/diluted
- **Wet cloves** put in straight after rinsing
- **A warm spot**, which speeds yeast up
- **Left a long time** — the longer it sits, the more fermentation

The CO₂ is why the jar fizzes, bubbles rise around the cloves, and the lid builds pressure.

## Is it safe?

For most people, a mildly alcoholic garlic honey is not a safety problem — this is the same process behind mead, and the alcohol level is low. It changes the **flavour**, which is the real complaint: boozy, winey, sometimes sharp.

Two caveats worth stating plainly:

- **The botulism question.** *Clostridium botulinum* spores occur naturally in honey and in soil on garlic. Botulism risk rises in low-acid, low-oxygen, watery conditions. A proper garlic honey ferment becomes **acidic** over time (lactic acid bacteria at work), which is the protective factor. Keeping the mix **honey-dominant** rather than watery, and giving it time to acidify, is the point of the ratio advice below. **Never give honey — fermented or not — to infants under 12 months.**
- **Alcohol content.** It's low, but it isn't zero. If you avoid alcohol entirely, or you're giving it to children, this matters.

If the jar smells **putrid, cheesy, like nail polish remover or rotten**, shows **fuzzy mould**, or the honey has gone **slimy or discoloured** — throw it out. Alcohol/cider smell is normal fermentation; rot is not.

## How to fix and prevent it

**For the jar you have now:**
- **Burp it daily** — unscrew the lid to release CO₂, then close it (pressure can pop lids)
- **Add more honey** to dilute the water back down, then stir/turn
- **Move it somewhere cooler**
- Use it where the boozy note works: marinades, glazes, dressings, roast vegetables, drizzled on cheese

**Next batch:**
- Use **more honey, fewer cloves** — the cloves should be fully submerged with honey over the top, not packed in
- **Dry the peeled cloves** thoroughly before they go in
- Use **raw honey** (its wild cultures help the lactic bacteria establish) but don't dilute it
- **Leave headspace** and expect to burp the jar daily for the first week or two
- Keep it at **room temperature, out of sun**, and turn the jar daily so the cloves stay coated
- Once it's as you like it, move it to the **fridge** to slow everything down

## What normal garlic honey does

- Cloves **float** at first, then sink as honey pulls water out
- Honey gets **thinner and runnier** — expected
- **Bubbles** for the first weeks
- Cloves turn **translucent and golden**, then mellow in flavour
- Garlic may turn **blue or green** — a harmless reaction between garlic's sulphur compounds and acid

## FAQ

**Why does my fermented garlic honey taste like wine?**
Yeast has fermented some of the sugars into alcohol, because the mixture became watery enough to allow it.

**Can I still eat it?**
If it smells of alcohol or cider and looks normal, most people use it as normal. Discard anything mouldy, slimy, or smelling putrid.

**How do I stop it fermenting further?**
Add more honey, burp the jar, and keep it in the fridge.

**Is garlic honey safe for babies?**
No. Honey in any form is not given to infants under 12 months.
