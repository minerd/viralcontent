# Why Is My Silkie Chick Sneezing? Dust, Crest Feathers and Real Respiratory Illness

A sneeze or two from a brooder chick is common. From a **silkie**, it deserves a closer look than it would from a hybrid layer — the breed has a reputation for respiratory trouble, and silkie chicks are small, slow-feathering and easily chilled.

**The practical rule: no discharge + normal eating and activity = usually environmental. Any discharge, noise or lethargy = treat it as illness.**

## The environmental causes (most sneezing)

**1. Dust**
Brooders are dusty: feed powder, pine shavings, chick down and dander. Dust irritates airways and produces sneezing and watery eyes in otherwise healthy chicks.
- Avoid very fine or dusty bedding; sift feed dust out
- Ventilate without draughting the chicks
- Don't use cedar shavings at all

**2. Crest and nostril feathers — the silkie-specific one**
Silkies have a **crest** that falls over the face and small feathers right at the nostrils. Both cause sneezing: feathers tickle the nares, and crest feathers get into the eyes. A chick that sneezes when it shakes its head, with clean nostrils, is often just dealing with its own feathers.

**3. Feed or water up the nose**
Chicks plunge their heads into feed and water. A sneeze straight after drinking is usually just that.

**4. Mould and damp bedding**
Mouldy litter or feed causes serious respiratory disease (aspergillosis) in young birds. Damp brooder corners are a real hazard, not a cosmetic issue.

**5. Stress**
Shipping, chilling, overheating and crowding all suppress the immune system and bring on respiratory signs.

## When it's illness

Treat as a health problem if you see **any** of:

- **Discharge** from nostrils or eyes, especially thick or bubbly
- **Gurgling, rattling, clicking or wheezing** breathing
- **Open-mouth breathing** or stretching the neck up
- **Swollen face or eyelids**, foam in the eye
- **Lethargy, fluffed posture, not eating**
- A **smell** to the breath
- Several chicks affected and getting worse

Silkies are commonly cited as more susceptible than other breeds, and respiratory infections in chicks move fast. A vet (or your country's poultry advisory service) is the right call — and a sick chick needs to be **separated** from the rest immediately, in a warm, clean, quiet brooder.

Be careful about reaching for medications bought online without a diagnosis: several of the common causes (mycoplasma, infectious bronchitis, aspergillosis) need different handling, and some of them make a bird a lifelong carrier for your whole flock.

## Brooder checklist

1. **Temperature**: chicks spread evenly, not piled under heat, not pressed to the walls panting
2. **Bedding**: dry, low-dust, changed often, never cedar, never mouldy
3. **Ventilation**: air movement above the chicks, no draught at their level
4. **Feed**: fresh, dry, stored sealed — mouldy feed is a direct cause
5. **Water**: clean, shallow, changed daily
6. **Space**: silkies are slow to feather and need the warmth longer than most chicks
7. **Quarantine**: never put new birds straight in with your chicks

## FAQ

**Is a sneezing chick always sick?**
No. Dust, bedding, feed and the silkie's own crest feathers all cause sneezing in healthy chicks.

**Why are silkies more prone to respiratory problems?**
They're widely reported as more susceptible, and their crest, small size and slow feathering make chilling and irritation easier.

**Should I give antibiotics for sneezing?**
Not on a guess. Get a diagnosis — the common causes are handled differently, and some have long-term flock implications.

**Can I keep a sneezing silkie chick with the others?**
If it has no discharge and behaves normally, yes, while you fix the dust and bedding. Any sign of real illness means separating it now.
