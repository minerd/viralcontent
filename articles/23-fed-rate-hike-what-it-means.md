# The Fed Just Raised Rates. What It Means for Your Credit Card, Mortgage and Savings

For most of this year, people were waiting for rates to go *down*. Instead, on September 16, 2026, the Federal Reserve raised them.

It was the first rate hike in more than three years. Officials voted unanimously to lift the benchmark rate by a quarter point, to a range of **3.75% to 4%**. The reason is inflation, which has picked up since early in the year, pushed along by the war with Iran and higher oil prices.

And the Fed hinted it might not be done. Its updated projections showed a strong majority of officials think another hike is possible before the end of the year.

Here's what it means for your money, in order of how fast you'll feel it.

## Credit cards: you'll feel it first

Most credit cards have variable rates tied to the Fed's rate. When the Fed goes up, card rates usually follow within one or two billing cycles.

A quarter point doesn't sound like much, and on a small balance it isn't. But if you carry a big balance month to month, it adds up, and more hikes would add up faster.

**What to do:**
- If you carry a balance, this is a good moment to attack it. Pay down the highest-rate card first.
- Look at 0% balance transfer offers, but read the transfer fee and the date the promo ends.
- Call your card company and ask for a lower rate. It doesn't always work. It works more often than people expect.

## Savings: good news, but slowly

Higher rates are good for savers. The catch is speed. Big banks tend to raise the rates they *charge* quickly and the rates they *pay* slowly.

**What to do:**
- If your savings sit in a big bank account paying almost nothing, it's worth comparing online banks and credit unions, which typically move faster.
- If you're thinking about a CD, rates may keep rising if the Fed hikes again. Some people split their money, locking some in now and keeping some flexible.

## Mortgages: it's complicated

Here's the part that surprises people: **mortgage rates don't directly follow the Fed.** Fixed mortgage rates track longer-term bond yields, which move on expectations about inflation and the economy.

That means mortgage rates might already reflect some of this, or might move in either direction.

**If you have a fixed-rate mortgage:** nothing changes. Your rate is locked.

**If you have an adjustable-rate mortgage (ARM) or a HELOC:** your rate will likely rise at the next adjustment. Check your statement for when that is.

**If you're shopping for a home:** don't try to time the Fed. Get quotes from several lenders, and ask about rate locks.

## Car loans and other borrowing

New car loans and personal loans will likely get a bit more expensive. If you already have a fixed-rate car loan, you're not affected.

If you're planning a big purchase on credit in the next few months, compare lenders, and consider whether a bigger down payment makes sense.

## Why is the Fed raising rates when everyone expected cuts?

Short version: prices started rising faster again. Higher oil prices feed into gas, shipping and eventually lots of other things. The Fed's main tool for slowing inflation is making borrowing more expensive, which cools spending.

It's painful if you carry debt. It's the Fed's bet that it's less painful than inflation that keeps climbing.

## A quick checklist for this week

1. Look at your credit card statements. Know your rate and your balance.
2. Check what your savings account actually pays.
3. If you have an ARM or HELOC, find your next adjustment date.
4. Hold off on new variable-rate debt unless you really need it.

## FAQ

**What is the Fed rate now?**
After the September 16, 2026 decision, the federal funds rate target range is 3.75% to 4%.

**Will the Fed raise rates again in 2026?**
Officials' projections suggest another hike is possible later this year, but it depends on inflation data.

**Do mortgage rates go up when the Fed raises rates?**
Not directly. Fixed mortgage rates follow longer-term bond markets, though Fed decisions influence them.

**Is now a good time to open a high-yield savings account?**
Rising rates generally help savers, so it's a reasonable time to compare options.
