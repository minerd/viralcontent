# Just Got Scammed? What to Do in the First Hour

First: take a breath. This happens to smart, careful people every day, and modern scams, especially the ones using AI voices and realistic fake messages, are built specifically to get past careful people.

Second: move fast. The first hour is when you have the best chance of stopping money from leaving, locking your accounts, and limiting the damage.

Here's the order to do things in. Don't skip ahead to "who do I report this to." Money and accounts first.

## Minute 0–10: Stop the money

**If you paid by bank transfer:** Call your bank now. Use the number on the back of your card or the official app, never a number from the scammer's messages. Say clearly: "I've been the victim of fraud and I want to stop or recall a payment." Some banks can freeze or recall transfers if you're fast enough.

**If you paid by card:** Call your card issuer. Ask them to block the card and dispute the charge. Card payments often have better fraud protection than transfers.

**If you used a payment app** (Zelle, PayPal, Venmo, Cash App, Revolut, etc.): Report it inside the app and contact your bank too, since the app is usually linked to your account.

**If you bought gift cards:** Call the company that issued the gift card (the number is usually on the back or on their site). Tell them the cards were used in a scam. Keep the cards and receipts.

**If you sent crypto:** Contact the exchange you sent from right away. Crypto is hard to recover, but exchanges can sometimes freeze funds that land on their platform.

## Minute 10–25: Lock your accounts

If you shared any login details, codes, or let someone "remote in" to your computer:

1. **Change your email password first.** Your email is the key to resetting everything else.
2. **Change passwords** for your bank and any account the scammer mentioned.
3. **Turn on two-factor authentication** wherever you can, using an app or your phone, not just email.
4. **Sign out of all sessions.** Most big services (Google, Apple, Microsoft, banks) have a "sign out everywhere" option.
5. **If someone had remote access to your computer:** Disconnect it from the internet and uninstall whatever they asked you to install. Get it checked before you use it for banking again.

If you shared a one-time code that came by text, assume they used it. Check that account first.

## Minute 25–40: Write everything down

While it's fresh, write down or screenshot:
- The phone number, email, or profile that contacted you
- What they said and asked for
- What you sent, how, and when (amounts, times, reference numbers)
- Any names, websites, or account numbers they gave you

Don't delete the messages. Your bank and the police will want them.

## Minute 40–60: Report it

Reporting helps you get your money back and helps stop the scammer hitting the next person.

- **Your bank or card provider** (if you haven't already opened a fraud case)
- **Local police:** many places let you file a fraud report online
- **Your country's fraud reporting service:** for example, the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov and the FBI's IC3 in the US, or Action Fraud in the UK. [ADD: your readers' main country's service if you have a specific audience]
- **The platform the scammer used:** report the account on WhatsApp, Instagram, Facebook, etc.

## After the first hour

**Watch your accounts closely** for a few weeks. Scammers sometimes come back.

**Beware of "recovery" scams.** This is a cruel one. After you've been scammed, someone may contact you offering to get your money back for a fee. That's almost always the same scammers, or new ones who bought your details. Real authorities don't charge you to recover money.

**Consider a credit freeze** if you shared your ID number, date of birth, or other personal details. It stops anyone from opening credit in your name.

**Tell someone.** A friend, a family member. Scams depend on shame and silence. Talking about it also helps protect people close to you.

## You're not stupid

It's worth saying one more time. Scams now use voices cloned from real family members, messages written by AI with no spelling mistakes, and fake websites that look perfect. They're designed by people who do this full-time, and they hit you when you're rushed, worried, or distracted.

Getting scammed doesn't mean you're gullible. It means you were targeted. What matters now is what you do next, and if you've followed this list, you've done it.

## FAQ

**Can I get my money back after a scam?**
Sometimes, especially with card payments and if you report quickly. Bank transfers and crypto are harder but not always impossible. Speed is the biggest factor.

**Should I call the scammer back?**
No. Don't engage further. Everything you need to do goes through your bank, the platform, and the authorities.

**Will my bank refund me?**
It depends on your country, your bank, and how the payment was made. Some countries have rules requiring banks to refund certain scam victims. Ask your bank directly.

**What if the scammer has my personal details?**
Change your passwords, turn on two-factor authentication, watch your accounts, and consider a credit freeze if they have ID details.
