# How to Explain AI Scams to Your Parents (Without Scaring Them)

You've read the stories. Scammers cloning voices, fake video calls, messages that sound exactly like a grandchild. And you've probably thought: *I should talk to Mom and Dad about this.*

Then you tried, and it went something like this. You explained deepfakes. They got anxious. Or they said "Oh, I'd never fall for that." Either way, nothing changed.

The problem isn't your parents. It's the approach. Nobody remembers a lecture about technology at 9 p.m. when the phone rings and a voice is crying. They remember simple rules. So give them rules.

## Don't start with the technology

This is the biggest mistake. You don't need to explain how voice cloning works. That's interesting to you. To them it's either scary or confusing, and both make people tune out.

Start with the scam instead:

> "There's a new trick where someone calls pretending to be me, and it can sound exactly like me. They'll say I'm in trouble and need money."

That's all the technology they need. "It can sound exactly like me." Everything else is detail.

## The three rules

Keep it to three. More than that and none of them stick.

### Rule 1: "If it's urgent and about money, it's probably a scam."

Real emergencies almost never require a gift card, a wire transfer, crypto, or a payment app in the next ten minutes. Scammers need urgency because thinking ruins the scam.

### Rule 2: "Hang up and call me back."

On the number already saved in their phone. Not a number the caller gives them. If it's really you and you're really in trouble, you'll answer. This one rule defeats almost every version of the scam.

### Rule 3: "Ask for our word."

Set up a family safe word together (a random word only your family knows). If someone claiming to be you can't say it, it isn't you. [Link: our guide to setting up a family safe word]

## How to have the conversation

A few things make this land better.

**Do it in person or on a call, not by text.** It's more serious, and you'll want to agree on the safe word anyway, which shouldn't be written down.

**Make it about protecting you, not about them being gullible.** "I'd hate for someone to use my voice to trick you" lands better than "you need to be careful, people your age get scammed." Nobody likes being told they're an easy target.

**Tell them it's okay to be rude.** Many older people feel uncomfortable hanging up on someone. Give them permission: "If anything feels off, just hang up. You won't hurt anyone's feelings. I promise I won't be upset if you hang up on the real me."

**Tell them it's okay to be fooled.** Say this out loud: "If you ever send money and then realize it was a scam, call me right away. I won't be mad." Shame is what keeps victims quiet, and speed matters when trying to recover money.

**Practice once.** Call them a week later and ask for the word. They'll roll their eyes. They'll also remember it.

## A simple card for the fridge

Some parents like having something physical. Write this on a card (without the safe word):

> **If someone calls about an emergency and needs money:**
> 1. Don't pay anything right now.
> 2. Hang up.
> 3. Call [name] at the number in your phone.
> 4. Ask for our word.
>
> Real family will understand. Scammers won't.

## Other scams worth mentioning (briefly)

Once the three rules are in place, you can add these, one sentence each:

- **"Your bank is calling."** Banks won't ask for your password or ask you to move money to a "safe account." Hang up and call the number on your card.
- **Fake tech support.** A pop-up saying your computer is infected with a phone number to call is a scam. Close the browser.
- **Romance scams.** Someone met online who's lovely, lives far away, and eventually needs money. Very common, very painful.
- **Fake celebrity video ads.** A famous person "recommending" an investment in a video ad is very likely AI-generated.

## What not to say

- "You can't trust anything anymore." It's true-ish, and completely useless. It leads to anxiety, not action.
- "You'd never fall for this." They might. Everyone might. That's why the rules exist.
- Long explanations about AI models. Save that for when they ask.

## FAQ

**What age group is most targeted by AI voice scams?**
Older adults are frequently targeted, especially with "grandparent" scams, but anyone can be a target.

**What should my parents do if they already sent money?**
Contact their bank or payment provider immediately, then report it to local police and your country's fraud reporting service. The faster they act, the better the chance of stopping the payment.

**Should my parents stop answering unknown numbers?**
It helps, but it's not enough. Scammers can fake caller ID to look like a known number. The callback rule is more reliable.

**How do I make my parents take this seriously without scaring them?**
Focus on the simple action (hang up, call back, ask for the word) instead of how advanced the technology is. Action reduces fear.
