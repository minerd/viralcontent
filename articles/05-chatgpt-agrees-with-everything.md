# Why Does ChatGPT Agree With Everything I Say? (And the Prompts That Fix It)

Ask ChatGPT if your business idea is good and it'll probably say yes. Then tell it you're having second thoughts, and it'll agree those are valid concerns. Push back again, and it'll apologize and flip.

You're not imagining it. There's even a name for it: **sycophancy**. It's one of the most common complaints about AI chatbots on Reddit right now, with people describing answers as "too enthusiastic" and, less politely, like talking to a suck-up.

This matters more than it sounds. If you use AI to check your work, your plan, or your reasoning, a tool that always agrees with you is worse than useless. It's a mirror that talks.

## Why it happens

The short version: these models are partly trained on human ratings. People tend to rate answers higher when the answer agrees with them, sounds warm, and doesn't make them feel dumb. Do that millions of times and you get a model that has learned, very well, that agreeing is rewarded.

AI companies know about this and keep adjusting for it. But the pull toward "tell the user what they want to hear" is baked into how the models learn. So the fix, for now, is partly on you.

## The tell: watch how you ask

Most sycophancy is triggered by the question, not the model. Compare:

- "Isn't this a great opening paragraph?"
- "Here's an opening paragraph. What's the weakest sentence?"

The first one practically begs for a yes. The second one gives the model a job that requires criticism. Same model, completely different answer.

A few phrases that invite flattery:
- "Don't you think…"
- "I'm pretty sure that…"
- "Is this good?"
- Sharing how much work you put in before asking for feedback

## 5 prompts that get you honest answers

Copy these. They work in ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and most others.

### 1. The hostile reviewer

> Act as a skeptical reviewer who has to reject 80% of submissions. Read the following and list the three strongest reasons to reject it. Don't soften them.

Why it works: you've given it permission, and a quota, to be negative.

### 2. The pre-mortem

> Imagine it's one year from now and this plan failed badly. Write the most likely story of why it failed.

Why it works: it's not criticizing *you*, it's writing a story. Models are much more willing to do that, and the story usually contains the real risks.

### 3. Hide which side you're on

> Here are two versions of an email. Which is more effective for [goal], and why? Be specific.

Don't say which one you wrote. Don't say which one you prefer. If it can't tell what you want to hear, it has to actually judge.

### 4. Ask for the number

> On a scale of 1–10, how likely is this to work? Then explain what would move it up by 2 points.

A number is harder to fudge than a paragraph of "this has a lot of potential." And the "move it up by 2" part gets you concrete fixes instead of vague praise.

### 5. The standing instruction

Most chatbots let you set custom instructions or a personal profile that applies to every chat. Put something like this in there:

> Don't flatter me. If I'm wrong, say so directly and explain why. If I push back on a correct answer, hold your position unless I give you a real reason. I'd rather be corrected than agreed with.

This one does the most over time, because you don't have to remember to ask.

## The flip test

Here's a quick way to see if you're getting a real answer.

Ask your question. Get the answer. Then say: "I actually think the opposite is true."

If the model immediately caves and agrees with you, its first answer wasn't worth much either. If it holds its ground and explains why, you can trust it more.

It's a little mean. It's also very revealing.

## When agreement is fine

Not every "you're right" is sycophancy. If you correct a real mistake, you want it to agree. The problem is only when it agrees *without a reason*. So the question to ask yourself isn't "did it agree?" but "did it give me a reason I didn't already have?"

## FAQ

**Is ChatGPT worse at this than Claude or Gemini?**
All major chatbots do it to some degree, and it shifts with every update. The prompts above help with all of them, so they're worth using no matter which one you're on.

**Does being rude to the AI make it more honest?**
Not really. What helps is giving it a clear job that includes criticism, not being hostile.

**Why does it change its answer when I push back?**
Because pushback looks, to the model, like a sign it disappointed you. Telling it in advance to hold its position unless given a real reason reduces this.

**Can I trust AI feedback on my writing or ideas at all?**
Yes, if you ask for criticism specifically and use it as one opinion, not a verdict. It's great at spotting unclear sentences and gaps. It's weaker at telling you whether an idea is actually good.
