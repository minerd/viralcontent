# Why Is My Emperor Scorpion Not Eating? Fasting, Molting and Temperature

Nearly every page about this is a forum thread. The short version keepers give there is worth stating plainly:

**Emperor scorpions fast, often for a long time, and a fasting scorpion in good condition is usually fine.** Weeks is common. A month or two is not unusual in adults.

## The normal reasons

**1. It's coming up to a molt (pre-molt)**
The classic cause. Before shedding, a scorpion stops eating, becomes reclusive, often stays in its burrow, and may look dull or slightly bloated. Juveniles do this repeatedly as they grow.

**Don't feed during pre-molt and don't feed for several days after a molt** — a freshly molted scorpion is soft and can be injured by prey. Remove live prey from the enclosure during this time.

**2. It simply isn't hungry**
Emperors are ambush predators with slow metabolisms. A well-fed adult can go a long time between meals with no ill effects. This is normal biology, not a problem to solve.

**3. Temperature is too low**
This is the most common husbandry cause. Cool scorpions become sluggish and stop feeding. Emperors want a warm enclosure — a warm end around the high 20s°C (low-to-mid 80s°F), not room temperature in a cold house. Use a thermostat and measure at substrate level, not on the wall.

**4. Humidity too low**
Emperors are a humid-forest species. Dry, dusty setups cause stress, molting problems and poor feeding. Deep, slightly damp (not wet) substrate they can burrow in, with good ventilation, is the target.

**5. Recently acquired or disturbed**
A newly imported or rehomed scorpion may not eat for weeks. Rehousing, frequent handling, rearranging the enclosure, or a tank in a high-traffic place all suppress feeding. Leave it alone.

**6. Prey it doesn't want**
Some individuals ignore crickets but take roaches or superworms. Try a different feeder before assuming illness. Also check prey size — very large prey can be refused.

**7. It's eating and you're not seeing it**
Emperors feed at night and often in the burrow. Count feeders in, and look for discarded chewed remains.

## When it isn't normal

Look at **condition and behaviour**, not the calendar:

- **Shrunken, wrinkled or collapsed look** to the body
- **Lethargy with no burrowing**, lying in the open, unresponsive
- **Twitching, uncoordinated movement, or curled legs** (classic signs of pesticide exposure or serious stress)
- **Mycosis**: dark, discoloured patches on the exoskeleton, often from an enclosure that's wet and badly ventilated
- **Mouthparts damaged** or stuck shed material around the chelicerae after a bad molt

Pesticide and chemical exposure is a real risk: no fly spray, flea treatment, air freshener or scented cleaner near the enclosure, and no wild-caught prey from treated areas.

## Checklist before you worry

1. Temperature at the warm end, measured at substrate level, on a thermostat
2. Humidity appropriate, substrate damp but not waterlogged, with ventilation
3. Deep substrate to burrow and a secure hide
4. Water dish, shallow, always available
5. Prey type varied; nothing live left in with a pre-molt animal
6. Enclosure left undisturbed, out of the way of traffic and vibration
7. Body condition and weight checked, not the feeding diary

If all seven are right and the animal looks plump and calm, waiting is the correct action.

## FAQ

**How long can an emperor scorpion go without eating?**
Weeks routinely, and adults can go a month or two. Condition matters far more than the interval.

**Should I feed a scorpion that just molted?**
Wait several days until the exoskeleton hardens, then offer small prey.

**Why won't my scorpion eat crickets?**
Individual preference is common. Try roaches or superworms, and make sure prey isn't too large.

**Is force-feeding ever a good idea?**
No. It risks injury, particularly near a molt.
