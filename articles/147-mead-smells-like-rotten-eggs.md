# Why Does My Mead Smell Like Rotten Eggs? Hydrogen Sulfide and How to Get Rid of It

You walk past your fermenter and get a whiff of **rotten eggs**. Or struck matches. Or, as one home brewer put it, "farts." It's alarming, but it's also one of the most common mead-making problems, and it's often fixable.

## What's causing it

That smell is usually **hydrogen sulfide (H2S)**, a gas produced by **stressed yeast**.

Yeast needs nutrients (especially nitrogen) to ferment sugar in a healthy way. Honey is famously **low in nutrients**. When yeast runs short, it can produce hydrogen sulfide.

**Common stress factors:**
- **Not enough yeast nutrient** (the most common cause)
- **Low pH**: honey is acidic, and adding acid early can push it lower
- **Temperature** too high or swinging a lot
- **Too little yeast** pitched
- **Some yeast strains** are simply more prone to producing sulfur

## What to do during fermentation

If you catch the smell **during active fermentation**:

**1. Add yeast nutrient**
If you haven't added nutrients, or haven't added enough, many brewers add nutrients to help the yeast recover. (Follow the product instructions and a trusted mead nutrient schedule; adding nutrients late in fermentation isn't always recommended.)

**2. Stir and degas**
Gently stirring the mead helps release trapped gas, including hydrogen sulfide, and can help the yeast. Stir carefully, as fermenting mead can foam up.

**3. Check temperature**
Move the fermenter to a stable spot within your yeast's recommended range.

**4. Check pH** (if you have the tools)
If pH is very low, some brewers adjust it, following reliable guidance.

## If the smell lingers after fermentation

- **Time**: a mild sulfur smell often fades as the mead ages and is racked
- **Racking**: transferring the mead off the sediment (lees) can help
- **Copper**: some brewers briefly expose the mead to **clean, sanitized copper** (for example, copper tubing or a copper scrubber), which can react with sulfur compounds. Research this carefully before trying it, and follow reputable homebrew guidance

## How to prevent it next time

- **Use yeast nutrients**, ideally added in stages early in fermentation
- **Rehydrate and pitch enough healthy yeast**
- **Control fermentation temperature**
- **Don't add acid** before fermentation unless your recipe clearly calls for it
- **Degas** gently in the first days of fermentation

## Is it ruined?

Not necessarily. Many meads that smelled of sulfur during fermentation turn out fine after the problem is addressed and the mead has time to age.

## FAQ

**Why does my mead smell like sulfur?**
Usually because stressed yeast is producing hydrogen sulfide, often due to a lack of nutrients.

**Will the rotten egg smell go away?**
Often, especially if you address yeast stress early and give the mead time.

**How do I fix sulfur smell in mead?**
Add nutrients during active fermentation (following guidance), stir to degas, control temperature, and allow time.

**Is mead with a sulfur smell safe?**
The smell usually signals yeast stress rather than danger, but judge the final product carefully before drinking.
