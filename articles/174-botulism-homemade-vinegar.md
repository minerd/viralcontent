# Can You Get Botulism From Homemade Vinegar? What the Real Risks Are

Making your own vinegar from wine, cider or fruit scraps is a fun, thrifty project. But botulism comes up whenever home food preservation is discussed, so it's worth understanding where the real risks are.

*This is general food safety information. Follow guidance from reliable food safety sources such as your national food safety agency or university extension services.*

## Why finished vinegar isn't a botulism risk by itself

Botulism is caused by toxins from *Clostridium botulinum*. The bacteria can grow and produce toxin in **low-acid, low-oxygen** conditions, which is why low-acid canned vegetables, meats and garlic-in-oil are known risks.

**Vinegar is highly acidic.** Food safety guidance commonly uses a pH of **4.6** as the dividing line: botulism bacteria don't grow in foods more acidic than that. Properly made vinegar is far more acidic than that threshold.

## The real risks with homemade vinegar

**1. Using homemade vinegar for canning or pickling**
This is the main issue. Home canning and pickling recipes are tested with **commercial vinegar of a known acidity** (commonly 5% acetic acid). Homemade vinegar's acidity varies and is usually unknown.

If you use weaker homemade vinegar in a canning recipe, the final food may not be acidic enough to be safe.

**Guidance commonly given by food safety experts:** use **commercial vinegar of the stated acidity** for canning and pickling. Save homemade vinegar for salad dressings, marinades and fresh uses.

**2. Garlic, herbs or vegetables stored in oil**
This is a separate, well-known botulism risk. Garlic or herbs in oil at room temperature can create low-acid, low-oxygen conditions. Food safety guidance generally recommends refrigerating such mixtures and using them quickly, or acidifying ingredients using tested methods.

**3. Infused vinegars**
Adding fresh garlic, herbs or peppers to vinegar is a common kitchen project. Keep infused vinegars refrigerated for quality and follow reliable recipes.

## Other things that can go wrong

Homemade vinegar can have other problems:
- **Mold** on the surface (fuzzy, colored spots): discard
- **Fruit flies** and contamination: cover with cloth
- **Weak, flat vinegar**: needs more time or more alcohol to convert

A **vinegar mother** (a slimy, jelly-like disc) is normal and not a sign of spoilage.

## FAQ

**Can homemade vinegar cause botulism?**
Finished vinegar is very acidic, which prevents botulism bacteria from growing. Risks come from using weak homemade vinegar for canning, and from garlic or herbs stored in oil.

**Can I use homemade vinegar for pickling?**
For canning and shelf-stable pickles, food safety guidance recommends commercial vinegar of known acidity. Homemade vinegar is best for fresh uses.

**What acidity is needed to prevent botulism?**
Foods with a pH of 4.6 or lower are considered high-acid for safety purposes.

**Is the vinegar mother safe?**
Yes. The mother is a normal, jelly-like mass of cellulose and bacteria in raw vinegar.
