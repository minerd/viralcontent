# Why Do My Zebra Finches Eat Their Eggs? Calcium, Stress and Over-Breeding

You check the nest and find a broken shell, a wet patch, or simply fewer eggs than yesterday. Zebra finches do eat their own eggs, and it's almost always a sign that something in the setup is off.

## The main causes

**1. Not enough calcium**
The commonest explanation. A hen that has just put calcium into a shell and has none left in her diet may eat the shell back. Birds laying clutch after clutch run down fast.

Provide, permanently:
- **Cuttlebone** in the cage
- **Mineral or grit block**
- **Egg food** (soft food) during laying and rearing
- Leafy greens; a **calcium/vitamin D3 supplement** if your vet advises

Calcium needs **vitamin D3** to be absorbed, so birds with no natural daylight or full-spectrum light can be short even with a cuttlebone present.

**2. Inexperience**
First-time pairs, especially young ones, often fail their first clutch — pushing eggs out, breaking them, or eating them. Many of these pairs go on to rear normally once they've matured. Zebra finches can breed from a very young age; letting them wait until they are properly mature (around a year) reduces failures.

**3. Exhaustion from over-breeding**
Zebra finches will breed back-to-back until they are wrecked. A pair rearing one clutch while incubating the next is short on energy, protein and calcium, and failures become routine.

Break the cycle: **remove the nest and nesting material** after one or two clutches and give the pair months off.

**4. Infertile or dead eggs**
A hen with clear, infertile eggs — including a lone hen laying with no male — may eat them. From the bird's side this is sensible recycling of nutrients rather than a vice.

**5. Disturbance and stress**
Repeated nest inspections, a cage in a busy walkway, other birds raiding the nest, bright light at night, predators at the window. Stressed finches abandon, kick out, or eat eggs.

**6. It becomes a habit**
Once a pair has done it, they may keep doing it even after the diet is fixed. This is why prevention matters more than correction.

## What to do, in order

1. Add **cuttlebone, mineral block and egg food** permanently — before the next clutch, not after.
2. Make sure there's **daylight or full-spectrum lighting** for D3.
3. Move the cage somewhere **quiet**, and stop inspecting the nest more than briefly.
4. House the pair **on their own** while breeding; colony cages invite egg loss.
5. Give **12 hours dark**, with a consistent routine.
6. If the pair has had two clutches, **remove the nest** and rest them.
7. For a persistent egg-eater, **stop breeding that pair**: no nest, no nesting material, shorter day length.

## What about fostering or dummy eggs?

Some breeders swap in plastic dummy eggs, or move eggs to a reliable foster pair (often society/Bengalese finches). Both are real options, but they treat the symptom. If the cause is diet or exhaustion, fix that first — and be aware that fostering has its own welfare and record-keeping downsides.

## When it's not egg-eating at all

- **Eggs vanish with no trace:** another bird in the cage, or they've been buried in nest material.
- **Eggs cracked but uneaten:** often clumsy heavy birds, a flimsy nest, or a nest site that tips.
- **Eggs thrown out whole:** a rejection behaviour, not eating, and usually points to disturbance or an unsuitable nest.

## FAQ

**Is egg-eating normal in zebra finches?**
It happens often enough to be familiar, but it's a signal — usually calcium, inexperience, exhaustion or stress — not something to accept.

**Will they stop if I give them cuttlebone?**
Often yes, if calcium was the cause and you provide it before the next clutch. Established habits can persist.

**Why would a single hen lay and then eat her eggs?**
Hens lay without a male, and those eggs are infertile. Eating them recovers the nutrients.

**How many clutches should I let a pair have?**
One or two, then remove the nest and give them an extended break.
