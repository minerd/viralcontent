# Stop Asking AI for Answers. Ask It What You're Missing.

Most people use AI like a search engine that talks. Question in, answer out.

That works for "how do I convert a PDF to Word." It works badly for anything that actually matters, like choosing a job offer, planning a move, or figuring out why your side project isn't getting users. For those, your question is usually the problem. You don't know what you don't know, so you ask the wrong thing, and the AI answers the wrong thing very confidently.

So flip it. Before you ask for an answer, ask what you're missing.

## The prompt

Here's the basic version:

> I'm trying to [goal]. Here's my situation: [2–5 sentences]. Before you give me any advice, what questions should I be asking that I'm not? What information would change your answer?

That's it. Nothing clever. But it changes the whole conversation.

Instead of getting a generic list of tips, you get things like: "Is the second offer remote? Does your current employer match 401(k)? Have you checked whether the higher salary moves you into a different tax situation?" Suddenly you're thinking about the decision instead of reading a listicle.

## Why this works better than a "perfect prompt"

AI models are good at knowing what's usually relevant to a type of problem. They've seen millions of versions of "should I take this job" or "why isn't my website ranking." What they don't know is *your* version.

A normal question makes the model guess the blanks in your situation, usually with the most average assumption. Asking "what am I missing" makes it show you the blanks, so you can fill them yourself.

It also dodges a known problem: chatbots tend to go along with how you framed things. If your question has a bad assumption baked in, the answer will too. Asking for missing questions pulls the assumption out into the open.

## Five ways to use it

### Big decisions

> I'm deciding between [A] and [B]. What are the questions people in my position usually regret not asking?

"Regret" is doing a lot of work here. It pushes the answer toward the stuff people learn the hard way.

### Work projects

> Here's my plan for [project]. What would a senior person in this field immediately ask me that I haven't addressed?

Great before a meeting with your boss, a client, or anyone who's going to poke holes in it anyway.

### Health and medical appointments

> I have a doctor's appointment about [symptom]. What questions should I ask the doctor, and what should I track before I go?

To be clear: this isn't asking AI for a diagnosis. It's using it to prepare so you get more out of the 15 minutes with an actual doctor. That's a very good use.

### Money

> I'm about to [buy a car / sign a lease / take a loan]. What terms or fees do people commonly overlook?

The answer won't know your contract. But it'll give you a checklist to read it with.

### Learning something new

> I want to learn [skill]. What do beginners usually not realize they need to learn first?

This one saves weeks. Most beginner frustration comes from a missing foundation nobody mentioned.

## Level two: make it interview you

Once you're comfortable with the basic prompt, try this:

> Ask me questions one at a time to understand my situation. After 5 questions, give me your advice.

This forces the back-and-forth that makes advice actually fit. It's slower. It's also the closest thing to talking to a smart friend who's actually listening.

## One warning

Asking better questions doesn't make the answers automatically correct. AI can still be wrong, outdated, or confidently make things up, especially about laws, prices, and anything that changed recently. Use it to find the right questions, then check the important answers with a real source or a real person.

That's the whole trick, honestly. Let the AI help you think. Don't let it think for you.

## FAQ

**Does this work with ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini?**
Yes. It's not a model-specific trick. It works with any decent chatbot.

**Isn't this just a longer prompt?**
It's a different kind of prompt. Instead of asking for output, you're asking for the gaps in your input. That's what makes the next answer better.

**What if the AI asks questions I can't answer?**
That's useful information too. It usually means you need to find something out before you decide.

**Can I use this at work without sharing private info?**
Yes. Describe your situation in general terms and leave out names, numbers, and anything confidential. The missing-questions part still works.
