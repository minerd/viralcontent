# Why Aren't My Diamond Doves Sitting on Their Eggs? Nerves, First Clutches and Nest Setup

Your pair built a nest, laid two eggs, sat for a couple of days — and then stopped. This is the single most common complaint from new diamond dove keepers, and the reasons are nearly always fixable.

## 1. They're nervous birds and something is disturbing them

Diamond doves are **markedly more nervous than larger doves**. A pair that sees people all day, or that gets checked on, usually will not incubate reliably.

Triggers that break incubation:
- Cage in a **busy walkway**, a kitchen, or a room with a TV close by
- **Nest inspections** — opening the cage, lifting the bird, moving the nest after eggs are laid
- **Any change** after the eggs are laid: a new cage position, new toy, cleaning the cage interior, rearranging perches
- Pets, especially cats, that can watch the cage
- Night disturbance: lights, noise, a mirror the bird sees movement in
- **Other birds** in the same cage or flight

What to do: put the cage somewhere **quiet and slightly elevated**, back it against a wall or cover one side, approach calmly, and leave it entirely alone during incubation. Feed and water from outside the cage if your setup allows.

## 2. First clutches usually fail

Experienced breeders treat a **first clutch as practice**. New pairs sit for a few days and quit, break eggs, or lay on the cage floor. That isn't a defect; it's inexperience, and most pairs settle after one or two attempts.

If a first clutch fails, let them try again. Don't change the setup dramatically in between — the lesson they need is repetition.

## 3. They can't leave the nest to eat and drink

Doves swap incubation duties and take short breaks. If **food, water and grit aren't easy to reach**, they will not stay on eggs. Keepers who have seen clutches abandoned very often find the pair had to travel too far, or compete with cage-mates, to feed.

Provide, close to the nest:
- Seed mix suitable for small doves
- **Grit** and a **cuttlebone/mineral source**
- Clean water
- A calcium source especially for the hen, who is laying again soon

## 4. The nest itself

- **Nest pan or basket** mounted high in the cage, not on the floor
- Open-fronted pans (plastic canary nest pans, wicker baskets) rather than enclosed boxes
- **Nesting material**: fine grass, coconut fibre, small twigs. Diamond doves build flimsy nests and eggs roll out of a flat, unlined pan
- A **wire or mesh base liner** so the nest keeps its cup
- One nest per pair, and not too many nest sites — too much choice causes them to shuffle between them

## 5. Infertility and clear eggs

Sometimes they sit for the normal period, nothing hatches, and then they abandon. That's a **fertility** issue, not a behaviour one:
- A single hen will lay without a male; those eggs are always clear
- Two hens will lay a nest of four clear eggs
- Young or very old birds, poor condition, or birds on a seed-only diet have poor fertility
- Check sex: diamond dove sexing is subtle, and misidentified pairs are common

Candling after a week tells you whether to bother waiting.

## 6. Too many clutches

Diamond doves will breed continuously if you let them, and worn-out pairs abandon. After **two or three clutches**, remove the nest and nesting material and let them rest for months.

## If the eggs are already abandoned

- You can let them go and wait for the next attempt — this is usually the right call
- Experienced keepers sometimes **foster** eggs under a steady pair of doves
- Hand-incubation and hand-rearing of dove chicks is difficult; it isn't a beginner rescue

## FAQ

**How long do diamond doves incubate?**
About two weeks. If they're still sitting past that with no hatch, the eggs were likely clear.

**Can I touch the eggs or move the nest?**
Avoid it. Interference after laying is one of the main reasons diamond doves abandon.

**Should I remove the eggs they abandoned?**
Yes, once you're sure they've quit — and if you don't want continuous breeding, remove the nest and material too.

**Will they lay again?**
Almost certainly, and soon. The question is whether you want them to; repeated clutches exhaust the hen.
