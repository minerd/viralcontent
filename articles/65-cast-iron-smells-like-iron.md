# Why Does My Cast Iron Skillet Smell Like Metal? (And Is It Safe?)

You heat up your cast iron skillet and there it is: a sharp, metallic, "blood-like" or coin-like smell. Maybe your food even tastes a bit like it.

**The usual reason: bare iron is exposed.** Good cast iron is protected by *seasoning*, thin layers of oil baked onto the surface. When that layer wears thin or gets damaged, you're smelling (and tasting) the iron itself.

It's rarely dangerous, and it's very fixable.

## The common causes

**1. Thin or worn seasoning**
New pans, pans that have been scrubbed hard, or pans that haven't been used much often have patchy seasoning. Gray or dull spots are a giveaway.

**2. Cooking something acidic**
Tomato sauce, wine, vinegar, citrus. Acidic foods, especially simmered for a long time, can strip seasoning and pull iron into the food. That's when you most often notice a metallic taste.

**3. Soaking or dishwasher damage**
Leaving cast iron to soak or putting it in the dishwasher can strip seasoning quickly.

**4. Rust**
Rust has its own sharp, metallic smell. Look for orange or reddish-brown spots.

**5. A stripped or restored pan**
If you've just stripped an old pan back to bare metal, it'll smell strongly metallic until it's re-seasoned.

## Is it safe?

For most people, yes. A little iron in food isn't harmful, and cast iron is widely used. The metallic taste is more of a quality problem than a safety one.

Two sensible exceptions:
- **Rust:** don't cook on active rust. Remove it first (see below).
- If you have a medical condition that requires you to limit iron intake, ask your doctor about cooking regularly in cast iron.

## How to fix it

**If it's just thin seasoning:**
1. Wash with warm water and a little soap if needed (modern mild dish soap won't hurt seasoning).
2. **Dry completely**, then put it on low heat for a few minutes to drive off moisture.
3. Rub a **very thin** layer of cooking oil all over, inside and out. Then wipe it off as if you made a mistake. What's left is plenty.
4. Heat it upside down in the oven at a high temperature (commonly around 450–500°F / 230–260°C) for about an hour. Put foil on the rack below to catch drips.
5. Let it cool in the oven. Repeat 2–3 times for a solid layer.

**If there's rust:**
1. Scrub the rust off with steel wool or a scrubber until you see gray metal.
2. Wash, dry thoroughly, and follow the seasoning steps above immediately. Bare iron rusts quickly.

**Then cook fatty foods for a while.** Frying, searing, cornbread and roasted vegetables with oil all build seasoning naturally. Hold off on long-simmered tomato sauces until the seasoning is solid.

## How to stop it coming back

- Dry your pan **immediately** after washing, ideally on the stove
- Wipe on a tiny bit of oil after each use
- Don't soak it or run it through the dishwasher
- Keep acidic cooking short, or use enameled cast iron for long simmers
- Store it dry, with the lid off or a paper towel inside if you stack pans

## Metallic vs rancid: know the difference

- **Metallic smell:** bare iron or rust. Fix the seasoning.
- **Rancid, stale or "old oil" smell:** too much oil left on the pan or old seasoning going off. Heat the pan to burn it off, or scrub and re-season with thinner layers.

## FAQ

**Why does my cast iron smell metallic?**
Usually because the seasoning is thin or damaged and bare iron is exposed, or because of rust.

**Is it safe to cook in cast iron that smells like metal?**
Generally yes, but remove any rust first and rebuild the seasoning for better-tasting food.

**Why does my food taste like metal from cast iron?**
Often because acidic food stripped the seasoning or the pan's seasoning is thin.

**How do I get rid of the metallic smell in cast iron?**
Clean, dry thoroughly, and re-season with several thin layers of oil baked on at high heat.
