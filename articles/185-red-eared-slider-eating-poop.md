# Why Do My Red-Eared Sliders Eat Poop? Coprophagy in Turtles Explained

You see one slider take a mouthful of another's droppings — or a hatchling going after its own. It's unpleasant to watch, and most pages answer the "its own poop" version of the question while skipping the tank-mate one.

**Eating droppings (coprophagy) is a normal, documented behaviour in turtles**, and in a mixed-age tank, the *other* turtle's droppings are the more useful target.

## Why they do it

**1. Seeding gut bacteria**
Young turtles are not born with a full set of gut microbes. Eating the droppings of an older turtle is one route to picking up the bacteria that help digest plant material. This is why it's more often **juveniles** doing it, and often aimed at an **adult's** droppings.

**2. Recovering nutrients**
Turtle digestion is not complete. Partly digested material still holds food value, and a scavenger takes it.

**3. Opportunistic scavenging**
Sliders investigate everything in the water with their mouths. Much of what looks like deliberate eating is tasting and spitting.

**4. Hunger or an unbalanced diet**
A turtle that's underfed, or kept on an all-pellet diet with no greens, scavenges more. A slider that eats droppings *and* gravel, plants and decorations is telling you it's looking for something.

**5. Boredom in a bare tank**
Nothing to forage, nothing to graze: droppings become the most interesting object available.

## Is it harmful?

Mostly it isn't — but in a closed tank it carries two real risks:

- **Parasite cycling.** If a turtle has intestinal parasites, eating droppings re-infects it and infects tank mates. A turtle that eats droppings and has runny, smelly or odd-looking faeces, or is losing weight, is worth a faecal test at a reptile vet.
- **Water quality.** Droppings left in the water to be picked over mean more ammonia. Over time that's the bigger health threat.

## What to change

1. **Filtration and water changes.** Sliders are messy. Over-filter heavily, and do regular partial water changes; test for ammonia and nitrite.
2. **Feed in a separate container** if you can, so uneaten food and the resulting mess stay out of the main tank.
3. **Give them something to graze.** Floating leafy greens (dandelion, romaine, water lettuce, duckweed) keep them busy and fed. A grazing turtle scavenges less.
4. **Balance the diet** by age: more protein for hatchlings and juveniles, far more plant material as they mature.
5. **Siphon visible droppings** promptly rather than letting them sit.
6. **Check stocking.** Several sliders in a tank sized for one produces both the mess and the behaviour.

## When to see a reptile vet

- Runny, bloody, pale or very foul droppings
- Weight loss, or a turtle that eats constantly but isn't growing
- Lethargy, swollen eyes, open-mouth breathing, soft shell
- Several turtles in the tank with poor droppings — suspect parasites and test

## FAQ

**Is it normal for turtles to eat their own poop?**
It's common and documented, especially in young turtles, and is linked to acquiring gut bacteria and recovering nutrients.

**Why does my baby slider eat the adult's poop?**
That's the clearest version of the behaviour: picking up gut flora from an older animal.

**Can eating poop make my turtle sick?**
By itself, usually not. It does recycle intestinal parasites if any are present, and it goes with poor water quality, which is the real risk.

**How do I stop it?**
Better filtration and faster waste removal, feeding outside the tank, and adding grazeable greens so they have something better to do.
