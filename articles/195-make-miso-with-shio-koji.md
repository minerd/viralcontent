# Can You Make Miso With Shio Koji? What Works and What Doesn't

Search this and you get recipes for **making shio koji** and recipes for **making miso**, but not the thing you asked: can one become the other?

**Short answer: no, not properly.** Shio koji is the wrong form of the same ingredient. But there are two things you *can* do, and the distinction is worth getting straight.

## What each thing actually is

**Koji** (*kome-koji*, rice koji): steamed rice inoculated with *Aspergillus oryzae* and grown until the grains are covered in white mycelium. This is the raw material. It's full of live enzymes and comes **dry** (dried koji) or **fresh**.

**Shio koji**: koji + **salt** + **water**, blended and left for a week or two until it becomes a soft, sweet-savoury paste or slurry. It's a finished **seasoning**, roughly 10% salt.

**Miso**: cooked **soybeans** (or other beans/grains) + **koji** + **salt**, mashed together and aged for months to years. A common home ratio is about **beans : koji : salt ≈ 1 : 1 : 0.5** by weight, though it varies widely by style.

So koji is an ingredient in both. Shio koji and miso are two different end products made *from* koji.

## Why shio koji doesn't work as miso's koji

1. **The salt is already in it, at the wrong ratio.** Miso's salt percentage is calculated against the total weight of beans + koji + salt. Shio koji brings its own salt and water, so you lose control of the number that governs whether your miso ferments safely and keeps.
2. **It's already wet.** Miso needs to be a firm paste you can pack down and keep nearly airless. A slurry gives you a loose, watery ferment that's much more prone to surface yeast and mould.
3. **Its enzymes have already been working.** Shio koji has spent weeks breaking down its own starches. Miso relies on those enzymes acting on **soybean protein and starch** over a long age. Starting with a partly spent culture changes what you get.
4. **Its microbial population has moved on.** A mature shio koji is a different community from fresh koji.

You'd get *a* fermented bean paste. You wouldn't get miso, and you'd have less control over salt — which in a months-long room-temperature ferment is what keeps it sound.

## What you can do instead

**1. Make miso with dry or fresh koji (the right way)**
Buy **dried rice koji** — the same product used to make shio koji at home. It keeps well, is widely sold, and is exactly what miso recipes mean by "koji". Weigh beans, koji and salt, mash, pack tightly into a clean container, weight the surface, and age it in a cool, dark place.

**2. Use shio koji as a miso-adjacent seasoning**
This is the genuinely useful swap. Shio koji does much of what miso does in cooking: salt plus glutamate plus enzymes. Use it:
- As a **marinade** for fish, chicken or pork (it tenderises — the enzymes break down protein)
- Instead of **salt** in dressings, soups and braises
- On **roasted vegetables** or mushrooms
- In place of miso where you want umami without the aged, dark flavour

Note it is **not** interchangeable 1:1 by volume with miso: it's wetter, saltier per spoon in some brands, milder in flavour, and far less complex. Start with less and taste.

**3. Keep its enzymes if you want them**
Shio koji's enzymes are deactivated by heat — commonly cited as above about **40°C (104°F)**. Use it raw in dressings and finishing if that's the point; use it as a marinade or seasoning if you just want the flavour.

## FAQ

**Is shio koji the same as miso?**
No. Both are made from koji, but shio koji is a salted koji seasoning and miso is an aged bean paste.

**Can I substitute shio koji for miso in a recipe?**
In cooking, often yes, for salt and umami — but adjust the quantity and expect a milder, less complex result. Not in a miso-making recipe.

**What do I need to make miso at home?**
Soybeans (or other beans), **dried or fresh rice koji**, salt, a clean container, a weight, and months of patience.

**Can I make shio koji from miso?**
No — the process only runs one way, from koji outward.
